In the early 1920s, a young Karl Popper noticed something that seemed obvious
once stated but had major consequences: the theories he found most impressive —
Einstein's general relativity — had a very different character from the theories
he found intellectually suspect — Freudian psychoanalysis and Adlerian
individual psychology.

What was the difference?

## The Problem Popper Was Solving

The **demarcation problem** is the question of what distinguishes scientific
from non-scientific theories. This is not the same as asking which theories
are *true*. Astrology might be false, but is it unscientific in some deeper
sense than just being wrong?

Popper's answer was: yes. And the distinction matters.

The prevailing view before Popper was **verificationism** — the idea that a
statement is scientifically meaningful if and only if it can be confirmed by
empirical observation. This was the view of the Vienna Circle and the logical
positivists.

Popper thought verificationism was backwards. He noticed that Freudian
psychoanalysis could, in principle, find confirmation in almost any human
behaviour. If a patient improves, that confirms the theory. If the patient
doesn't improve, that confirms the theory too (the patient wasn't cooperating,
or the resistance itself was evidence of unconscious dynamics).

A theory that is consistent with every possible observation is not confirmed
by any observation. It is unfalsifiable — and, Popper argued, that is what
makes it non-science.

## Falsifiability as the Criterion

Popper's proposal: **a theory is scientific if and only if there exist
observations that could, in principle, show it to be false.**

Einstein's general relativity made a highly specific, easily falsifiable
prediction: the path of light would be bent by gravity in a precise, calculable
amount. If Eddington's 1919 solar eclipse expedition had found no bending —
or the wrong amount of bending — the theory would have been refuted.

The fact that the prediction was confirmed didn't *prove* the theory (no
amount of confirmation can do that, as Hume noted). But the *possibility* of
falsification is what made it scientific in the first place.

Compare this to a claim like "God works in mysterious ways." No possible
observation can falsify this, because any observation can be accommodated. It
is not meaningless — it may be deeply important to the person who holds it —
but it is not scientific.

## The Logic Behind It

Popper's insight connects to a basic asymmetry in logic.

You cannot prove a universal statement by observation. No matter how many
white swans you see, you cannot prove "all swans are white" — there might be
a black swan in Australia (there is). But you *can* disprove it: one black
swan refutes the claim.

This asymmetry — modus ponens doesn't give you universal truths; modus tollens
can give you universal falsifications — is the logical foundation of Popper's
criterion. Science proceeds not by accumulating confirmations, but by
subjecting theories to severe tests and retaining only those that survive.

## Where the Theory Succeeds

Falsifiability is genuinely useful as a practical heuristic.

When evaluating a theory or hearing an argument, asking "what would have to
be true for this to be wrong?" is almost always illuminating. If the answer
is "nothing could prove this wrong," that should trigger scepticism.

Falsifiability also correctly identifies what is admirable about the best
scientific theories: they stick their necks out. They make specific,
testable predictions and stand or fall by them.

## Where It Falls Short

The theory has well-known difficulties.

**The Duhem-Quine problem.** In practice, no theory is tested in isolation.
Every test rests on a web of auxiliary assumptions: about the accuracy of
instruments, background theories, calibration procedures. When an experiment
fails, you can always save the central hypothesis by rejecting an auxiliary
assumption instead.

Eddington's measurement came with its own uncertainties. If the result had
been slightly off, physicists might have questioned the observation conditions
rather than abandoned relativity. This flexibility is not unscientific — it is
rational. But it means the clean picture of theories being straightforwardly
falsified by single experiments is too simple.

**Historical cases.** The history of science is full of cases where a theory
survived apparently decisive disconfirmation. Newtonian mechanics was
apparently refuted by the precession of Mercury's perihelion, and astronomers
postulated a hidden planet (Vulcan) to save it. This was not crazy — the same
strategy had revealed Neptune. It turned out to be wrong. But the strategy
was reasonable.

**Mathematics and logic.** Pure mathematics is not falsifiable (it doesn't
make empirical claims) but is clearly not non-science in any important sense.
Falsifiability is a criterion for *empirical* science specifically.

## Lakatos and the Refinement

Imre Lakatos refined Popper's account into the theory of **scientific research
programmes**. On his view, what we should evaluate is not single theories but
programmes — a core set of commitments surrounded by a "protective belt" of
auxiliary hypotheses.

A progressive research programme is one where modifications to the protective
belt (to accommodate anomalies) generate new testable predictions, and those
predictions are confirmed. A degenerative programme is one where modifications
are purely ad hoc — made to avoid falsification without generating any new
insight.

This is more historically accurate and more forgiving of the local anomalies
that any scientific theory faces. But it is also vaguer, and harder to apply
in real time.

## The Enduring Value

Popper's core insight remains sound: science is distinguished not by
accumulating confirmations but by engaging seriously with the possibility
of being wrong.

The willingness to specify what evidence would change your mind, to construct
experiments that could falsify rather than merely confirm, and to treat negative
results as informative rather than inconvenient — these are hallmarks of
scientific integrity, even if Popper's criterion cannot be applied mechanically.

In a world full of theories that are framed to resist all possible
disconfirmation, this is still a valuable lesson.
